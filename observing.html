Observing the Night Sky

You don't need a big telescope to start. Here is how to begin.

Easy targets with the naked eye

  • The Moon: craters and dark plains are visible even through binoculars.
  • Planets: Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn look like bright "stars" that don't twinkle much.
  • The Milky Way: a faint band of light, visible only under a really dark sky.
  • Meteor showers: the Perseids (around 12 August) and the Geminids (mid-December) are among the best.

What you need

  • Your eyes: free, and surprisingly good once they adapt to the dark.
  • Binoculars: a pair like 10x50 shows the Moon's craters and Jupiter's four biggest moons.
  • A small telescope: great for Saturn's rings, but only worth buying after you know the sky.
  • A red flashlight: red light doesn't ruin your night vision.
  • A star map or app: helps you find what you are looking at.

Tips for beginners

  1. Get away from city lights if you can.
  2. Give your eyes 20 to 30 minutes to adapt to the dark, and avoid looking at your phone.
  3. Check the cloud forecast before going out.
  4. Dress warmer than you think you need to. Standing still at night gets cold fast.

Light pollution

Light pollution is artificial light that shines up into the sky. It washes out faint objects like the Milky Way and most stars.

In a big city you may see only a few hundred stars. Under a dark rural sky you can see thousands.

It also affects animals, for example birds and insects that use natural light to navigate.
